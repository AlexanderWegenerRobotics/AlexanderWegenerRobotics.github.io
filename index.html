The system

One person, the whole loop.

Collecting demonstrations, curating them into a dataset, training a policy, and putting that policy back on the hardware are usually four teams. Here they are one pipeline, designed and operated end to end — which is what makes the research question below answerable at all.

Collect

An interface built for hands that are already busy.

Driving two arms occupies both hands, so the interface cannot ask for them. Menus are selected by gaze or by voice; the wrist-camera picture-in-picture enlarges when the operator looks at it and shrinks when they look away. Attention drives the layout instead of a controller button.

Getting a frame across an ocean

Frames are read from shared memory — from the camera, or from MuJoCo, through the same buffer. A 64-bit wall-clock timestamp is embedded in an extra image row before encoding, so the receiver measures true one-way delay at decode time rather than assuming half the round trip. Encoding is GStreamer H.264 at ultra-low-latency settings, streamed as RTP over UDP with ULP-FEC redundancy, across a ZeroTier link.

When the link misbehaves

On the public internet, packet loss and jitter are operating conditions rather than failures. The sender runs a hysteresis-based state machine on the receiver's 50 Hz feedback and steps the stream down, trading bitrate and frame rate against forward error correction.

NORMAL→DEGRADED→CRITICAL→STALE

Hysteresis on the thresholds keeps the system from flapping between states when loss hovers near a boundary.

Curate

Labelled while it is collected, not afterwards.

At the end of every episode the operator is asked to mark it success, partial, or failure, and intent and phase labels are written as the episode runs. Nothing is reconstructed in post. Failed episodes are kept and marked rather than quietly dropped, because a dataset that only contains successes cannot tell you what failure looks like.

Small on purpose

A hundred and twenty episodes is not a foundation-model corpus and is not presented as one. It is a curated set with known provenance, documented failures, and labels produced at collection time — which is the property the research below depends on.

Train

Two models, built in the order that earns them.

Intent recognition

Predicts what the operator is about to do — which object, which arm, which destination, and which phase of the task — from gaze, arm state, and object positions. The representation is deliberately task-agnostic, so it carries across sorting, stacking, and assembly rather than being hand-built for one task.

Complexity is earned rather than assumed: a hidden Markov baseline first, then a GRU, then a transformer only where the simpler models demonstrably fall short.

Manipulation policy

An action-chunking transformer (ACT) trained on the collected sorting demonstrations, then deployed back onto the physical arms. This is the step that turns the pipeline from data infrastructure into a working autonomous skill, and it is what makes "did the demonstrations get better" a measurable question rather than a rhetorical one.

ACT after Zhao et al., Learning Fine-Grained Bimanual Manipulation with Low-Cost Hardware, RSS 2023.

Research contribution

Guiding the operator, and asking what it is worth.

Shaping what the operator sees changes how they work. The open question is whether it changes what their demonstrations can teach.

Established — MSc thesis, TU Munich

Nudging measurably improves the operator.

A ten-participant study across two tasks — gearbox assembly and tight-clearance tactile insertion — compared six feedback conditions from fully manual to combined informational and dynamic assistance. Roughly three hundred trials, randomised order, NASA-TLX after every trial, contact forces and torques logged from the robot.

Assisted conditions reduced perceived workload by twenty to forty TLX points on the insertion task, cut peak contact forces, and raised success rates without costing completion time. Notably, combining informational and dynamic assistance was not better than either alone — more help is not monotonically better.

Does it improve the policies too?

Better operators are not the point. Better data is. The current system predicts operator intent in real time and uses that prediction to shape what the operator sees — the ghost overlay, predictive cues — so the demonstrations they produce are cleaner and more consistent.

The test is a paired comparison: two policies with identical architecture and training budget, one trained on nudge-assisted demonstrations and one on unassisted, evaluated on task success. That reframes a noisy human-factors question as a single number a robot-learning team already cares about.

Gaze as input, never as label

Using the same signal to predict and to define ground truth would invalidate the result.

Deployment-available signals only

No privileged simulator state, so nothing works in sim that cannot work on hardware.

Complexity earned incrementally

A deterministic baseline first, and a larger model only where the simpler one demonstrably fails.
